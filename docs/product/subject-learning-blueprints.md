# Subject Learning Blueprints

## Why this document exists

The curriculum can share a common shape—subject, topic, sub-topic—without forcing every subject to teach or quiz in the same way. The product should share navigation, progress, and feedback patterns, but use subject-specific instructional blueprints for lessons, practice, and evidence of mastery.

These are starting recommendations, not final curriculum. The exact exam and its blueprint must be confirmed before writing the full subject maps.

## Shared learning loop, different teaching methods

Every learning unit can use the same outer loop:

1. State one clear learning objective.
2. Teach using a method suited to the subject.
3. Demonstrate how to think through an example or source.
4. Let the learner practice with feedback.
5. Check the skill with a short formative quiz.
6. Offer targeted review or another practice set when useful.
7. Check transfer and retention later in a mixed topic assessment.

The screen flow can stay familiar while the learning activities inside it vary. The three-variation math lesson pattern should not be the required template for science, language, reading, or reasoning.

## Cross-subject exam strategies

Teach test-taking strategies as a second layer alongside subject knowledge. They help learners make a more informed choice when unsure, but they do not replace learning the underlying material. Each tip should explain **when it helps, how to apply it, and when it can fail**.

Examples include:

- **Process of elimination:** remove choices that contradict the question, passage evidence, units, sign, grammar, or a rule the learner has already established. Then compare the remaining choices.
- **Check the question carefully:** notice qualifiers such as *except*, *most likely*, units, requested precision, and what the item is actually asking.
- **Estimate or test choices:** in suitable math problems, estimate a reasonable range, substitute answer choices, or work backward from a choice. Explain when this is faster than solving directly.
- **Use evidence:** in reading, language, and science questions, connect an option to the relevant passage, sentence, diagram, data, or concept instead of choosing what merely sounds familiar.
- **Manage time:** set a reasonable time limit, skip a question temporarily when stuck, and return if time allows. Teach this in timed practice and mocks.
- **Make an informed guess:** first check the target exam's scoring rules. If incorrect answers are penalized, the best choice after elimination can differ from an exam with no penalty. Do not recommend blind guessing as a universal rule.

For practice feedback, it can be useful to show both the subject-matter solution and a faster test-taking route. For a timed mock, preserve exam conditions and show strategy feedback after the attempt. Tag strategy tips with conditions and relevant item types so the app can surface them when useful.

## Draft subject blueprints

### Mathematics

**Learning target:** understand a mathematical relationship and apply a method to unfamiliar problems, not only repeat a demonstrated procedure.

**Suggested lesson pattern:**

1. Explain the concept with intuition, a diagram, or a concrete model where it helps.
2. Work through a clear example while naming each decision.
3. Fade hints across guided practice so the learner does more of the work.
4. Vary numbers, signs, representations, and context where those variations matter for the target skill.
5. End with an application or transfer problem and explain common misconceptions.

For some algebra skills, positive values, negative values, and fractions are useful variations; they are not a universal sequence for every math topic. Quizzes should tag the skill and error type so feedback can recommend relevant practice.

**Evidence of learning:** solve a new item, choose and justify a method, or apply the skill in a word problem. Include routine fluency and reasoning/application items in the topic blueprint.

### Science

**Learning target:** recall essential terms and facts, understand how ideas and processes relate, and use those relationships to explain or predict outcomes.

**Suggested lesson pattern:**

1. Introduce the key vocabulary in context.
2. Explain a concept, model, system, or cause-and-effect process.
3. Use a diagram, sequence, comparison, or worked interpretation when it helps make relationships visible.
4. Use brief retrieval practice for important facts and terminology.
5. Ask learners to interpret a diagram or data, predict an outcome, or apply an idea to a new situation.

Do not reduce science to fact memorization alone. The balance of factual recall, concept explanation, and application should follow the chosen exam.

**Evidence of learning:** retrieve important information and use it to explain, distinguish, interpret, or predict something in an unfamiliar example.

### Language proficiency / English

**Learning target:** understand and apply a language skill in context, such as grammar, vocabulary, sentence structure, or usage.

**Suggested lesson pattern:**

1. State the specific language skill and show it in a natural example.
2. Contrast correct usage with plausible errors.
3. Explain the rule or context that distinguishes them.
4. Give short editing, completion, or selection practice with explanations for each distractor.
5. Use later mixed questions to check whether the learner can recognize the skill in a different context.

**Evidence of learning:** select, edit, or interpret language correctly in a new sentence or passage context. Feedback should explain why the tempting alternative is wrong, not only show the right choice.

### Reading comprehension

**Learning target:** understand a passage and support an answer with evidence from it, including direct details, main idea, inference, tone, and vocabulary in context where relevant.

**Suggested lesson pattern:**

1. Model a reading move using a short passage and a clearly stated question.
2. Show how to locate or infer the answer from evidence in the text.
3. Explain why attractive distractors are unsupported, too broad, or contradicted by the passage.
4. Practice with varied passage types and question types.
5. Introduce timing strategies only after accuracy and evidence-based reasoning are understood.

**Evidence of learning:** answer questions on a new passage and identify the text evidence or reasoning that supports each answer.

### General reasoning and patterns

**Learning target:** identify relevant features, form a rule or hypothesis, test it, and apply the reasoning to a new problem.

**Suggested lesson pattern:**

1. Compare examples and name candidate features or relationships.
2. Form a possible rule and check it against all of the evidence.
3. Compare alternatives and explain why they fit less well.
4. Practice varied problems so learners do not memorize one visual or numerical pattern.
5. Teach a speed strategy only with its conditions and a check that guards against a tempting false pattern.

**Evidence of learning:** solve a new pattern or reasoning item and recognize when a proposed rule does not fit. Avoid teaching tricks as unexplained answers.

"General" can become too broad to teach consistently. Before mapping it, split it into the skill families that actually occur in the target exam, such as logical reasoning, data interpretation, quantitative reasoning, or figural patterns, if those are in scope.

## Make the curriculum and question bank teachable

For every sub-topic, define:

- a learner-facing objective;
- prerequisite knowledge;
- the teaching method and activity types for its subject;
- the misconceptions or error types to watch for;
- the question tags / skills the item bank needs;
- what a short practice quiz can diagnose;
- what a later mixed assessment should prove;
- the source or subject reviewer responsible for checking correctness.

Question tags should describe the skill being tested, not only the broad subject. A wrong answer can guide practice when its question has a useful skill tag and a reviewed explanation.

## Pilot strategy

Do not invent a complete teaching method for every subject before testing any of them. Select the first target exam and one pilot subject. Write its blueprint, create one complete sub-topic, and observe learners using the static-data prototype. Then refine that subject's blueprint. For each additional subject, adapt the method and validate a representative sub-topic before scaling its content.
